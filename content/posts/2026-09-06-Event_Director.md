Anyone actually reading this site will have quickly realised that a lot of my weekends revolve around parkrun. Not just running it but also volunteering. So it might be no surprise that I've been the Event Director at a junior parkrun for around six years and was on the core team for 3 years before that helping to set up the event. 

When I started, I don't think I thought of it as leadership experience but I have learnt so much over the years. 

I was volunteering. I was helping to organise a weekly event for children. There were practical things that needed doing, volunteers that needed organising and the occasional problem that needed solving.

But looking back now, I'm starting to realise just how much I've learned about leadership through those six years.

I've recruited and managed volunteers, dealt with complaints, carried out risk assessments, made decisions about whether an event was safe to go ahead, worked with the local council, managed budgets and supplies, spoken at local schools, developed volunteers ranging from Duke of Edinburgh students to older adults, dealt with incidents and communicated with a huge variety of people.

And I've done most of it because it needed doing and a great bonus is that I love the values of why I am doing it.

That, perhaps, is one of the things I've learned about leadership.

Sometimes you don't get to choose the leadership experience. You just have to step up and deal with the problem in front of you.

## Starting Again After Lockdown

One of the biggest challenges came after lockdowns.

Like many community activities, junior parkrun had to stop. When we were eventually able to restart, the team we had before lockdown had largely disappeared.

In fact, I was basically the only person from the original core team still involved, other than my wife who often opted to stay at home with our daughter who doesn't actually like running. 

So we had to start again.

That meant rebuilding a core team, finding volunteers and gradually getting the event back to something resembling what it had been before. Some weeks we actually had more volunteers than kids, they were hard!

It wasn't a grand strategic exercise.

There wasn't a leadership development programme.

It was simply a case of finding people who wanted to help, working out what needed doing and building a team around that.

But that experience taught me a lot about the importance of people.

You can't run a community event by yourself.

More importantly, you shouldn't try to. Whilst it can still be hard to get the roster full or people that want to be RD, it isn't as much work for me as it used to be. 

## Leadership Isn't Doing Everything Yourself

One of the things I've become much more comfortable with over the years is that being responsible for something doesn't mean doing everything yourself. This is both the case at work and at home, delegation and knowing your own strengths and weaknesses are crucial. I know some people who have refused to admit they have weaknesses and they were actually the least competent person I have worked with.  

As Event Director, ultimately I'm responsible for the event.

But that doesn't mean I should be the person doing every job.

A successful junior parkrun relies on volunteers doing lots of different things. Some people are comfortable speaking to large groups. Some are brilliant at encouraging children. Some are organised and reliable. Some are technically minded. Some just turn up every week and quietly get on with whatever needs doing.

My job is partly to understand those differences and put people in positions where they can contribute.

That's probably one of the most transferable leadership skills I've developed.

You don't need everyone to be the same.

You need to understand what people are good at and help them contribute.

## Communicating With Completely Different People

Over six years, I've worked with an enormous range of people.

I've had Duke of Edinburgh students volunteering with us for the first time. I've worked with older volunteers who have decades more life experience than me. I've dealt with parents, children, regular volunteers, occasional volunteers, members of the public, council staff and the parkrun organisation itself.

They all require slightly different communication.

A 12-year-old doing their first volunteering role probably doesn't need the same conversation as someone who has been volunteering for twenty years.

A parent who is worried about their child needs something different from a volunteer who has arrived early and just wants to know where they need to be.

That sounds obvious, but I think it is an important part of leadership.

**Communication isn't just about what you say. It's about understanding who you're communicating with.**

I've also had to become much more comfortable speaking publicly. I have given presentations in front of adults before (look up Dr Gary Manley on YouTube, not I am not a Dr) but speaking in front of kids is a different art. 

I've now presented at local primary schools, talked to groups of volunteers and regularly communicate with people before and during events. The parkrun primary scheme (hard not to type schema!) gas seen me visit many schools to talk about how awesome junior parkrun is and how the children can get involve, it is actually great fun. 

None of these things were necessarily skills I set out to develop.

They just became necessary.

## Developing Other People

One of the things I enjoy most about junior parkrun is seeing people develop.

That includes volunteers.

Someone might turn up as a Duke of Edinburgh student and have very little experience of volunteering. Over time, they become confident enough to take on more responsibility.

I've seen people go from needing quite a lot of direction to becoming people I can rely on to run an important part of the event without me needing to worry about it.

That's a really rewarding part of leadership. You haven't just got something done. You've helped someone else become capable of doing it.

The best leader isn't necessarily the person who can do everything themselves, it's the person who can help other people become capable of doing things themselves.

## Making Difficult Decisions

One of the less enjoyable parts of being responsible for an event is occasionally having to make a decision that people won't like.

Weather is a good example.

Junior parkrun is an outdoor event involving children, so there are occasions when the safest decision is to cancel. 40mph winds on the prom is not safe for a 4 year old, for example. I've had to make those calls.

It isn't particularly difficult to cancel when the weather is obviously dangerous. The harder decisions are when conditions are borderline.

People have made plans. Children are excited. Volunteers have arranged their Sunday morning around the event. But ultimately, someone has to make the decision.

And sometimes leadership means accepting that you might disappoint people because you've made the decision you believe is right. That is something I think applies equally in the workplace. You can't always make the popular decision, you have to make the appropriate one.

## Risk and Responsibility

Running an event for children also makes risk management very real.

I've completed risk assessments, considered changing weather and ground conditions (ice!), dealt with incidents and made decisions about whether an event can safely operate. There are also practical responsibilities that aren't necessarily visible to participants.

We need appropriate equipment and supplies. We have an AED. We need to think about what happens if something goes wrong. Incidents need to be reported to parkrun HQ. A lot of leadership is making sure that the things nobody notices **don't go wrong**.

## Dealing With Complaints

Not everyone is going to agree with every decision.

I've had to deal with complaints and difficult conversations with members of the public and parents. Again, I think the biggest lesson has been that the objective isn't necessarily to "win" the conversation. It also helps that there are a lots of policies in place for parkrun, and we always do our best to follow those and therefore can use those to explain the situation. FYI, dogs still aren't allowed at junior parkrun.

Sometimes people simply want to be heard.

Sometimes there is a legitimate issue that needs addressing.

Sometimes there is a misunderstanding.

And sometimes you have to explain why a decision was made even though the person you're talking to still doesn't agree with it.

I've become much more comfortable with those conversations over time.

## Working With People Outside the Team

The event doesn't operate in isolation either.

I've had to communicate with the local council around approval and the use of the location.

You need to build relationships with people who have different responsibilities and priorities. That feels very similar to what happens in professional environments.

A data team might be responsible for delivering something, but they rarely control every part of the environment around them.

There are stakeholders, governance requirements, budgets, other teams and competing priorities.

You have to work with those people rather than simply telling them what you want.

## The Operational Side of Leadership

There is also a surprisingly large amount of operational management involved.

I've managed budgets and supplies, dealt with fund-raising and made sure that the equipment required to run the event is available. This all sounds boring but is essential for the event to take place and can sometimes actually be quite interesting. 

## The Reason It Is Worthwhile

For all of the organisational and leadership lessons, there is one reason that keeps me volunteering.

The children and how powerful parkrun is for good health. 

There is one particular child who has made a lasting impression on me.

When they first started coming to junior parkrun, they used a buggy for sections of the course. Technically that isn't allowed but this is a disabled individual and their parents were always ensuring everyone was safe and fun came first. 

Over time, they progressed from using the buggy to walking more of the course, and eventually to running it.

Watching that progression has been incredible.

I remember one particularly cold winter morning when only five children turned up.

It would have been very easy to look at the numbers and think the event wasn't really worth all the effort.

But that child was there.

And they finished with a smile on their face. 

That was one of those moments where you remember exactly why you are doing it.

Six years of organising volunteers, completing paperwork, managing risks, dealing with complaints, sorting equipment and making difficult decisions can sound like a lot of work.

And it is.

But seeing a child progress from needing a buggy to running the course makes it worthwhile.

Now, there are often more than 100 children taking part. The contrast with that winter morning, when just five children turned up, is quite remarkable. And I think that experience has taught me something important about leadership too.

**The numbers aren't always the most important measure of success.**

## What Junior parkrun Has Taught Me About Leadership

Looking back, I think six years as an Event Director has given me experience in areas that I didn't necessarily associate with leadership when I started.

I've learned about:

- Building and rebuilding teams
- Recruiting and retaining volunteers
- Communicating with very different people
- Coaching and developing others
- Risk assessment and safety
- Making difficult decisions
- Handling complaints and conflict
- Stakeholder management
- Budget and resource management
- Governance and incident reporting
- Public speaking
- Operational planning
- Taking responsibility when things go wrong

But perhaps more importantly, I've learned that leadership isn't really about having a title.

It is about taking responsibility.

It is about helping other people succeed.

It is about making decisions when there isn't an obvious answer.

It is about communicating when things are difficult.

And it is about understanding that the people you're responsible for are not all the same.

## Bringing That Into Data Leadership

I'm still developing my understanding of what being a good data leader actually means.

I work in a technical data role, so naturally a lot of my experience has been around technology, data engineering and solving technical problems.

But I increasingly think that the technical side is only part of the picture.

A good data leader needs to understand technology, but also needs to understand people.

They need to communicate with people who don't necessarily understand the technology.

They need to build teams.

They need to develop people.

They need to manage risk.

They need to work with stakeholders who have different priorities.

They need to make difficult decisions.

And ultimately, they need to take responsibility for the outcome.

When I think about it that way, I know I've been getting some useful leadership experience for the last six years.
